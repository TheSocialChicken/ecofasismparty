What follows is the Official Account. Alternative accounts exist. Their authors are on sabbatical in rewilding labour camps and unavailable for comment. The Republic values accuracy. The Republic also values silence, when accuracy is inconvenient.

Part I — The Age of Burning (Before Year Zero)

The world the old generation built was a furnace with a loyalty programme.

They knew. The reports came in for sixty years — meticulous, damning, increasingly desperate. Each report was filed. Each committee discussed it. Each summit produced a communiqué. The communiqués were filed next to the reports. The burning continued.

By the mid-2020s, the language of climate discourse had fractured into two dialects: the performative (“we are deeply committed to net zero”) and the operational (drilling permits, new airports, fossil fuel subsidies). The gap between them was measured in parts per million.

And then the activists came out of the tunnels.

2024 — 2026

The Movement Expands. What began as spray paint and road blockades becomes a distributed network of cells operating across 34 countries. Membership swells after the Marseille Heat Event kills 6,000 in a fortnight. The public mood shifts. The spray paint stays on the buildings longer before anyone bothers to remove it.

2027 — The Great Burning

Southern European wildfires combine with record Saharan heat to produce a climate refugee crisis involving 4 million people in eight weeks. The Italian government collapses. Spain declares emergency rule. The Iberian network — the largest coordinated cell of eco-activists on the continent — begins operating openly, no longer bothering to wear masks. Nobody arrests them. The police have been reassigned to manage the refugee flows.

2028 — The London Protocols

A hung Parliament, a constitutional crisis, and three successive Prime Ministers in four months create the conditions. The movement’s legal arm — by now staffed with barristers, economists, and one former cabinet minister who’d had enough — drafts the London Protocols: emergency climate governance legislation, radical in scope, technically legal. It passes on a vote of 221 to 218. The margin is three MPs who believe it will fail quickly and position them well for the resulting election. It does not fail.

2029 — The Emergence of The Verdant

No one knows the original name. That’s the point. A figure appears simultaneously across the movement’s distributed cells — same manifesto, same cadence, same willingness to say the unsayable. “We have been asking. We have been asking for fifty years. The asking is over.” Whether The Verdant is one person, a committee, or an AI trained on climate grief and revolutionary tracts remains classified under the Green Covenant’s Article 12 (State Security Through Productive Ambiguity).

2029 — The Green Unification

The cells coalesce into the Green Order over a single extraordinary fortnight. Broadcasting House is occupied — not stormed, occupied, with the building’s own security standing aside after a three-hour conversation about their children’s future. Parliament follows. The elected members are invited to continue meeting, under supervision, as an advisory body. Most do. The ones who don’t are not harmed. They are simply ignored, which feels worse.

Year Zero — The Declaration

The Emerald Republic is declared from the steps of what was once the Palace of Westminster. The Carbon Constitution is ratified within six weeks. It is 14 pages long — shorter than most terms-and-conditions agreements — and contains within it the legal architecture for everything that follows. Legal scholars will later note that it was, in its way, an elegant document. Several of those scholars are currently on sabbatical.

Year I — The Necessary Unpleasantness

The Republic implements. Carbon rationing. Travel permits. The Extinction Tribunals — at first presented as truth commissions, evolving, as these things do, into something with rather more teeth. The private vehicle ban. The Great Rewilding Labour Conscription (three months mandatory service per adult citizen, per year). GreenCast replaces the BBC. The old internet is throttled to 2010 speeds to reduce data-centre power consumption; the dark web thrives. Nostalgia for the old world becomes a Category C offence.

Year II — Now

The Republic endures. The Earth, one must admit, does appear to be breathing slightly better. At what cost is a question you may ask quietly, in private, among trusted friends. At a party, say. A themed party. Where everything is fictional. Yes.

Part II — The Verdant: A Portrait

“I am not important. The carbon budget is important. I am merely the person who finally stopped being polite about it.” — The Verdant, First Address, Year Zero

What is known:

  • Appeared first as a pseudonymous author of the Extinction Manifesto (2026), which circulated on encrypted networks before being printed and left on the seats of trains across three continents
  • Voice: precise, unhurried, with the particular calm of someone who has already made peace with being called a monster
  • Never photographed without the Green Order’s sigil obscuring their face
  • Reportedly has strong opinions about compost
  • Two biographies have been attempted. One author is rewilding in Northumberland. The other recanted.

The cult of personality that has formed around The Verdant is, officially, discouraged. Unofficially, the murals are very good.
